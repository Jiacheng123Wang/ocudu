# Neural receivers trained END-TO-END with the channel decoder in the loop — a survey of the line and of the practical problems reported

**Scope.** Replace the whole NR uplink physical-layer detection chain (resource grid → soft bits / LLRs) with an AI model, deployed on Apple-Silicon GPUs (CoreML/Metal) in a real over-the-air gNB testbed.
**Survey date:** 2026-10-07.
**Companion document:** `ai_receiver_literature_review.md` (broader PHY-ML review). This file is the deep dive on *decoder-in-the-loop training* and on *what actually goes wrong*.

## 1. Classic and modern end-to-end autoencoder systems with coding in the loop

### 1.1 The foundational autoencoder framing

**An Introduction to Deep Learning for the Physical Layer** — IEEE Trans. Cognitive Communications and Networking 3(4):563–575, 2017 — [arXiv:1702.00832](https://arxiv.org/abs/1702.00832) — O'Shea & Hoydis.
Introduces the "communications system as autoencoder" framing: transmitter and receiver as NNs trained jointly through a differentiable channel. For a (7,4) Hamming-coded BPSK link the autoencoder approaches maximum-likelihood decoding of Hamming (7,4) and beats uncoded BPSK; for an (8,8) system it outperforms uncoded BPSK across the whole E_b/N_0 range, i.e. it **learned joint coding and modulation**. There is *no explicit channel decoder in the loop* here — coding is absorbed implicitly, which is exactly the assumption later work attacks. **[verified]**
Practically important for us: the paper already names two of the five problem categories. It observes that "it is not obvious at which SNR(s) an ML communications system should be trained… **Training at low SNR for instance does not allow for the discovery of higher structure important in higher SNR scenarios**", and it flags the choice of input representation — "plain channel observations or **(clipped) log-likelihood ratios**" — as an open design question. **[verified]**

### 1.2 The over-the-air ancestor

**Deep Learning-Based Communication Over the Air** — IEEE JSTSP 12(1):132–143, 2018 — [arXiv:1707.03384](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.03384) — Dörner, Cammerer, Hoydis, ten Brink.
**Attribution correction: this is not an O'Shea paper** — the title is often mis-attributed. It is the ETH/Bell-Labs prototype. It builds, trains and runs a complete NN-only communications system on **unsynchronized off-the-shelf SDRs** (USRP B200 TX, USRP B210 RX, 46 m indoor LOS hallway, NVIDIA TITAN X for training/finetuning), with a two-phase strategy: pretrain the autoencoder on a stochastic channel model, then **finetune the receiver on over-the-air recordings**. Performance comes "close to the performance of a well-designed conventional system" but the autoencoder is ~1 dB worse than the GNR DQPSK baseline across the full E_b/N_0 range. **[verified]**
The reported practical problem is a textbook outer-loop failure: finetuning on sequences for which the initial receiver had BLER worse than ~10⁻² improved low-SNR performance, "However, it also led to a **high error floor in the high SNR range**. Since finetuning with very noisy sequences obscures important structure in the data, **the receiver looses its ability to generalize to high SNR**." Finetuning on BLER < 10⁻⁴ sequences gave only marginal gain; training stopped early because "the NN started to overfit". **[verified]**

### 1.3 The clearest "decoder in the loop" architecture

**Trainable Communication Systems: Concepts and Prototype** — IEEE Trans. Communications, 2020 — [arXiv:1911.13055](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.13055) — Cammerer, Aït Aoudia, Dörner, Stark, Hoydis, ten Brink.
This is the single most directly relevant classical paper: it presents a **fully differentiable neural IDD (iterative demapping and decoding) structure** that unfolds the demapper plus an LDPC belief-propagation decoder over I iterations into one feed-forward network, and trains mapper + demapper end-to-end with a **standard 802.11n LDPC code (n = 1944)** in the loop. I = 40 iterations during training, same demapper weights reused at every iteration so the parameter count does not grow with iteration count. **[verified]**
Three findings matter for us:

* **Training instability is real and was solved with a multi-loss.** "It was experimentally observed that **training the end-to-end system by minimizing (12) [a loss computed only on the final decoder output] leads to poor performance.**" They had to sum the binary cross-entropy *at the demapper output at every one of the 40 iterations*. **[verified]**
* **The decoder shapes the LLRs, and the code then has to be re-matched to those LLRs.** They perform EXIT-chart-based degree-profile matching using the *measured* demapper LLR distributions, and impose a stability condition λ₂ ≤ β because otherwise "these intersections may translate into a **non-negligible error-floor or BER performance degradation**." **[verified]**
* **Measured, not simulated.** Two USRP B210s at 2.35 GHz / 15.94 MHz effective bandwidth, OFDM with 64 subcarriers, all schemes using IDD with 80 iterations. Over the real channel the learned demapper gains **0.6 dB over an AWGN-MAP demapper**, the autoencoder holds ~**1.0 dB** over QAM baselines, and LDPC code optimization measured from *over-the-air* EXIT curves adds **0.2 dB (m = 6) and 0.4 dB (m = 8)**. **[verified]**

### 1.4 Model-free training when the channel is not differentiable

**Model-free Training of End-to-end Communication Systems** — IEEE JSAC, 2019 — [arXiv:1812.05929](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.05929) — Aït Aoudia & Hoydis.
Alternating training: receiver with the true gradient, transmitter with an approximated gradient obtained by relaxing the transmit symbols with Gaussian perturbation and applying the log-trick. This removes the requirement of a differentiable channel model and works with non-differentiable components such as quantization. **[verified]**
Two reported costs. First, the perturbation variance σ_w "controls a tradeoff between the accuracy of the loss function gradient approximation and the estimator variance" — reducing σ_w makes the gradient estimate *more* accurate but higher-variance, "causing slow convergence". Second, and directly relevant to our SNR-generalisation question: "as in [27], **a separate NN is trained for each SNR value as the trained NNs struggle to generalize to other SNR values**." Their own testbed is a single USRP B210 at 2.3 GHz / 5 MHz, 128 subcarriers; they state that the main difficulty of the earlier prototype [the 2018 OTA paper] "was the difference between the [model and the real channel]". **[verified]**

### 1.5 Learned decoders — unrolled belief propagation and its training problems

**Learning to Decode Linear Codes Using Deep Learning** — Allerton Conf. 2016 — [arXiv:1607.04793](https://arxiv.org/abs/1607.04793) — Nachmani, Be'ery, Burshtein.
**Deep Learning Methods for Improved Decoding of Linear Codes** — IEEE JSTSP 12(1):119–131, 2018 — [arXiv:1706.07043](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.07043) — Nachmani, Marciano, Lugosch, Gross, Burshtein, Be'ery.
The learned-BP line: represent L full BP iterations as a trellis NN whose hidden layers correspond to Tanner-graph edges, and assign a trainable weight to each edge. Because of the code's symmetry it suffices to train on a single (all-zero) codeword. The JSTSP paper reports the earlier weighted-BP result of **0.9 dB improvement in the high-SNR regime**, extends it to min-sum, shows that **tying parameters across iterations** (an RNN) gives comparable results with far fewer parameters, and introduces a successive-relaxation recurrent decoder. Codes: BCH(63,45), BCH(63,36), BCH(127,64), BCH(127,99). They explicitly report **no overfitting** (train/validation/test results identical). **[verified]**
Gradient problem, stated plainly: for min-sum, "it is **not possible to use gradient descent to optimize these min-sum decoders, since the function computed by check nodes has non-differentiable kinks** at certain points. However, we have found that it is possible to instead use **subgradient descent**." **[verified]**

**Neural Offset Min-Sum Decoding** — IEEE ISIT 2017 — [arXiv:1701.05931](https://arxiv.org/abs/1701.05931) — Lugosch & Gross.
Replaces the single global offset β of offset min-sum with a **trainable offset per check-node degree**, β_d. Notable for our error-floor section: they record that per-degree offsetting "not only matches the performance of SPA but **can also lower the BER error floor exhibited by some codes**" — i.e. error floors are a first-class concern in the learned-decoder literature, not an afterthought. **[verified]**

**Deep Learning for Decoding of Linear Codes — A Syndrome-Based Approach** — IEEE ISIT 2018 — [arXiv:1802.04741](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.04741) — Bennatan, Choukroun, Kisilev.
Motivated explicitly by the generalisation failure of learned decoders: because the number of codewords is exponential in block length, "it is impossible to account for even a small fraction of them during training, **leading to poor generalization of the network to new codewords**. This issue was **a major obstacle** in [4,5], constraining their networks to very short block lengths." Their fix is to feed the network only the **syndrome of the hard decisions plus the channel reliabilities**, for which they "prove analytically that this approach does not involve any intrinsic performance penalty, and **guarantees the generalization of performance obtained during training**." Architecture is a stacked GRU RNN (4 levels, hidden state size 5N, 5 time steps, tanh). **[verified]**

**Learning to Decode Protograph LDPC Codes** — IEEE JSAC, 2021 — [arXiv:2102.03828](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.03828) — Dai, Tan, Si, Niu, Chen, Poor, Cui.
Neural min-sum for protograph LDPC with parameters shared per *edge type*. The paper is a catalogue of the deep-unrolling training problem: unrolling 25–50 decoding iterations "corresponds to quite deep neural networks. It may lead to the **vanishing gradient problem** during the training process. These reasons lead to that current neural SP decoders may only support short to moderate codelengths." Their remedy is **iteration-by-iteration greedy training** (train the last iteration, freeze, extend), which also reduces training complexity and mitigates message correlation from short cycles. They validate with trajectory-based EXIT (T-EXIT) charts. **[verified]**

**Boosted Neural Decoders: Achieving Extreme Reliability of LDPC Codes for 6G Networks** — IEEE JSAC, 2025 — [arXiv:2405.13413](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.13413) — Kwak, Yun, Kim, Kim, No.
Targets the thing an NR gNB actually cares about: xURLLC needs FER below 10⁻⁹, but 5G-NR LDPC codes "suffer from the error floor phenomenon". Their boosted neural min-sum decoder adds (i) boosting learning with *uncorrected vectors*, (ii) a **block-wise training schedule to address the vanishing gradient issue**, (iii) dynamic weight sharing, (iv) transfer learning, (v) data augmentation. Quantified training pathology: with one-shot training over 50 unrolled iterations, "the vanishing gradient problem **impedes the effective training of early iteration weights**. This results in a **consistent test FER up to iteration 40** for ℓ̄ = 50"; the block-wise schedule with Δ₁ = 5, Δ₂ = 10 is optimal and is what closes the gap. **[verified]**

### 1.6 Neural decoders for sequential/feedback codes

**Communication Algorithms via Deep Learning** — ICLR 2018 — [arXiv:1805.09317](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.09317) — Kim, Jiang, Rana, Kannan, Oh, Viswanath.
RNN decoders for convolutional and turbo codes reaching close-to-optimal AWGN performance (i.e. near Viterbi / BCJR), with "**strong generalizations, i.e., we train at a specific signal to noise ratio and block length but test at a wide range of these quantities**, as well as robustness and adaptivity to deviations from the AWGN setting." **[verified]**

**Deepcode: Feedback Codes via Deep Learning** — NeurIPS 2018 — [arXiv:1807.00801](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.00801) — Kim, Jiang, Kannan, Oh, Viswanath.
RNN encoder/decoder for channels with noisy, delayed feedback; at rate 1/3, K = 50, n = 150 they report codes that "**outperform known codes by 3 orders of magnitude in reliability**", with generalization to longer block lengths by unrolling the RNN cells, composability with turbo codes, and robustness to K-step feedback delay. **[verified]**

### 1.7 Other coding-in-the-loop and learned-decoder work

**Deep Learning for Channel Coding via Neural Mutual Information Estimation** — IEEE SPAWC 2019 — [arXiv:1903.02865](https://arxiv.org/abs/1903.02865) — Fritschek, Schaefer, Wunder.
Instead of back-propagating through a channel, they train the encoder by **maximising a neural estimate of mutual information**, which removes the need for the channel density. Background framing useful for us: they note E2E encoder-decoder systems "can achieve bit error rates which come close to practical baseline techniques **if they are used for over-the-air transmissions**", citing the 2018 OTA prototype. **[verified]**

**Deep Ensemble of Weighted Viterbi Decoders for Tail-Biting Convolutional Codes** — Entropy 23(1):93, 2021 — [arXiv:2009.02591](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.02591) — Raviv, Schwartz, Be'ery.
Parameterises the branch metrics *and* path metrics of the circular Viterbi algorithm (unfolded as an NN) and trains an **ensemble of 8 specialised decoders with 3 repetitions each**. Two details matter for our LLR section: the exact state-equality criterion "is non-differentiable, thus we minimize a multi-class cross entropy loss, **acting as a surrogate loss**", and their hyper-parameter table lists an explicit **"LLR clipping" parameter λ_max = 20**. They verify generalisation to two longer codes with ~0.6 dB FER gain preserved. **[verified]**

**Machine LLRning: Learning to Softly Demodulate** — IEEE SPAWC 2019 — [arXiv:1907.01512](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.01512) — Shental & Hoydis.
LLRnet: a trainable universal NN demodulator that maps received symbols to bit LLRs. For QAM it gives LLR estimates "approaching the optimal log maximum a-posteriori inference with **an order of magnitude less operations**". Validated in 5G-NR and DVB-S.2 link-level simulations. This is the "learned demapper feeding a conventional decoder" pattern, as opposed to the full detector replacement. **[verified]**

## 2. Neural receivers with LDPC in the loop, 5G-NR-PUSCH shaped

### 2.1 Nokia Bell Labs: DeepRx

**DeepRx: Fully Convolutional Deep Learning Receiver** — IEEE Trans. Wireless Communications 20(6):3925–3940, 2021 — [arXiv:2005.01494](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.01494) — Honkala, Korpi, Huttunen.
A preactivation-ResNet CNN that maps the frequency-domain resource grid (received data + known pilots + raw channel estimates) directly to **bit LLRs**, trained with sigmoid binary cross-entropy, with those LLRs fed to a **5G-compliant LDPC decoder** (rate dematcher included). Reported: coded BER **matches LMMSE with full channel knowledge**, roughly **2 dB** better than a practical LMMSE receiver with two pilots; with only one pilot the practical LMMSE "does not even reach the waterfall region of the code" while DeepRx retains nearly the same performance. Under strong interference it beats even the full-channel-knowledge LMMSE by **2 dB**, which they attribute to the LMMSE demapper assuming Gaussian white noise. **[verified]**
Two design facts we should copy. First, the modulation-order solution: DeepRx emits **8 output channels per resource element with hierarchical bit masking**, so **one network serves QPSK/16-QAM/64-QAM/256-QAM with no retraining**. Second, they treat LLR quality as a first-class metric: "**Investigating the coded BER reveals whether the LLRs provided by the DeepRx sufficiently capture the uncertainty of the detected bits for the purposes of LDPC decoding.** The coded BER can also be seen as a proxy to the accuracy of the LLRs themselves." **[verified]**

**DeepRx MIMO: Convolutional MIMO Detection with Learned Multiplicative Transformations** — IEEE ICC 2021 — [arXiv:2010.16283](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.16283) — Korpi, Honkala, Huttunen, Starck.
Extends DeepRx to 16 RX × 4 TX, 4-layer MU-MIMO, trained over CDL-A/TDL-E with RMS delay spread 10–300 ns, Doppler 0–325 Hz, SNR −4…32 dB, **16-QAM fixed**, code rate 658/1024. This is **the clearest published error-floor statement for a neural receiver with an LDPC decoder in the loop**: with two pilots it "can nearly match the uncoded BER of the genie-aided LMMSE up to SNRs of 14 dB, **after which it seems to encounter a BER floor**." They argue the floor is harmless *for that code rate* because the waterfall sits at low SNR — and then concede in the conclusion: "Our future work will include **further investigations into the error floor of the proposed MIMO DeepRx, which hinders its performance at very low bit error rates**." **[verified]**

### 2.2 Nokia Bell Labs / Sionna: 5G-NR MU-MIMO neural receiver

**A Neural Receiver for 5G NR Multi-user MIMO** — IEEE GLOBECOM Workshops 2023 — [arXiv:2312.02601](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.02601) — Cammerer, Aït Aoudia, Hoydis, Oeldemann, Roessler, Mayer, Keller.
A 5G-NR-PUSCH-compatible MU-MIMO neural receiver in Sionna, with a CNN state-initialisation plus message-passing iterations, flexible in number of users and layers, trained with BCE loss over randomized 3GPP UMi channels with random user drops "to ensure that the learned receiver generalizes to unseen channel conditions". It outperforms the LS baseline in all scenarios and approaches LMMSE + K-best detection at much lower complexity; in their results "**the error floor is significantly lower with the neural receiver**", which they read as implicit accurate channel estimation. They also ran a **hardware-in-the-loop 3GPP conformance test** (R&S SMW200A vector signal generator + R&S MSR4 receiver, 217 PRBs, TDL-B100/400 and TDL-C300/100, 2 users × 2 TX, 4 RX). **[verified]**
The two admitted gaps are exactly two of our problem categories: "**The flexibility w.r.t. the modulation schemes is an interesting and important challenge.** A possible approach for such a flexibility could be in input embeddings that account for the actual modulation scheme", and "We leave it open for future work to **optimize the architecture towards real-time inference**." **[verified]**

**Design of a Standard-Compliant Real-Time Neural Receiver for 5G NR** — [arXiv:2409.02912](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.02912) (2024, CENTRIC/NVIDIA) — Wiesmayr, Cammerer, Aït Aoudia, Hoydis, Zakrzewski, Keller.
The deployment paper. Contributions: (a) an adaptive **Var-MCS NRX** that supports **dynamic MCS without retraining and without additional inference cost** by masking unused higher-order LLR outputs (exploiting the hierarchical QAM bit structure); (b) latency optimisation to **under 1 ms inference on an NVIDIA A100 using TensorRT**, where "these latency constraints effectively limit the size of the NN" and cost "**less than 0.7 dB**" SNR relative to a preliminary non-real-time NRX; (c) a **multi-loss** training scheme that lets the number of unrolled iterations — and therefore the inference latency — be chosen *after* training without retraining; (d) site-specific adaptation with ray-tracing data. **[verified]**
It also contains the crispest statement of the LLR-mismatch problem for masked/mismatched demapping: "Though such mismatched demapping will produce LLRs with the same sign as a matched demapper for the correct constellation, **the LLR magnitudes do in general not match the underlying probabilities**… Without additional LLR correction, … 'classical' mismatched demapping can lead to **severe performance degradation in forward error correction (FEC) decoding**." **[verified]**
Additional cost datapoint from the **NVIDIA `neural_rx`** release ([github.com/NVlabs/neural_rx](https://github.com/NVlabs/neural_rx)): the reference NRX is *TensorRT/ONNX-exported* and the repo advertises "Real-Time Inference of 5G NR Multi-user MIMO Neural Receivers" with 5G-NR-compliant MU-MIMO PUSCH, 3GPP channel training, site-specific ray-tracing training, and end-to-end learning of custom constellations for pilotless communication. **[verified, README]**

### 2.3 Bell Labs: end-to-end OFDM and pilotless transmission

**End-to-end Learning for OFDM: From Neural Receivers to Pilotless Communication** — IEEE Trans. Wireless Communications 21(2):1049–1063, 2022 — [arXiv:2009.05261](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.05261) — Aït Aoudia & Hoydis.
Neural receiver operating over multiple subcarriers and OFDM symbols, then joint transmitter/receiver optimisation that **removes orthogonal pilots entirely**. Reported: significant BER gains at high mobility and with sparse pilots; end-to-end learning gives **4 %–8 % additional throughput** depending on scenario by eliminating DMRS; PAPR of the learned constellations is comparable to conventional QAM. **[verified]**
Important boundary for our line: they compare against an **IEDD** (iterative demapping/decoding) baseline and state that their neural receiver achieves its gains "**although the channel code is not at all leveraged as for IEDD**" — i.e. their headline receiver does **not** use decoder feedback. This is a useful statement that decoder-free neural detection already captures most of the gain in *that* setting, and it sets up the question of when the decoder in the loop is actually worth it. **[verified]**

### 2.4 NVIDIA / ETH Zurich: real-time, standards-compliant, measured

**On the Impact of Site-Specific Training for a Real-World 5G NR System** — [arXiv:2609.04004](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.04004) (Sept 2026) — Wiesmayr, Baytekin, Dick, Studer.
This is the strongest *measured* result set in the whole survey and the only one that puts a **decoder-in-the-loop receiver** on a real 5G NR testbed. Setup: the standard-compliant 5G NR testbed at ETH Zurich (NVIDIA Aerial Testbed software, COTS Open RAN radio units, 100 MHz at 3.45 GHz, 273 PRBs, 30 kHz SCS, TDD pattern 3DSU), **COTS UEs** (Samsung Galaxy S23, Apple iPhone 14 Pro, Google Pixel 9 Pro) carried along random trajectories, two indoor scenarios, and **two measurement campaigns more than six months apart** (Nov 2025, Jun 2026). Three architectures are compared: a fully trainable neural receiver (**NRX**, CGNN, ~1.2·10⁵ shallow / 4.4·10⁵ deep parameters), a model-driven neural receiver (**MDX**, ~2.5·10³ parameters), and a model-based **deep-unfolded iterative detection and decoding (DUIDD)** receiver with **only 30 tunable parameters** and I = 2 MMSE-PIC stages with N_MP = 12 LDPC message-passing iterations. Crucially: "**The DUIDD receiver — in contrast to the NRX and the MDX — includes the LDPC MP decoder during tuning.**" **[verified]**
Numbers: site-specific finetuning reduces dataset BLER by about one third (0.351 → 0.237) in the small laboratory and nearly one half (0.463 → 0.240) on the office floor for MDX; NRX dual-layer finetuning reduces the effective SNR needed for 25 % dataset BLER by **0.83 dB (shallow) and 0.54 dB (deep)**; a model finetuned on one campaign still beats the pretrained model on the other campaign, but the temporal gap increases BLER. **Decoder-in-the-loop finetuning gains almost nothing**: "Site-specific finetuning of DUIDD, however, reduces the dataset BLER by only another **0.004 in absolute terms**. This marginal gain suggests that the receiver's **limited tunability leaves little room for further finetuning**." Both NRX configurations beat DUIDD but at much higher complexity. Meanwhile, **site-specific LMMSE channel estimation combined with iterative detection and decoding achieves the lowest error rate observed in their datasets** — i.e. in the measured regime, a well-tuned classical IDD chain beats the neural receivers. **[verified]**

### 2.5 The calibration-drift benchmark — the most important recent paper for this workstream

**When Does a Neural Receiver Help? Calibration-Drift Benchmarking and Detect-and-Rollback for 5G/6G NR** — [arXiv:2605.26157](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26157) (May 2026) — Elnashar.
A 16-scenario out-of-distribution benchmark of the **public MathWorks `DeepRx_2M` reference model** (1.23 M parameters, 26 PRBs, SIMO 1×2, 3.5 GHz, 16-QAM, rate 658/1024, transport block 10504 bits, LDPC decoder in the loop, 200 slots per SNR point, −2…+18 dB), sweeping seven channel models (CDL-B/C/D, TDL-B/C/D/E), four Doppler shifts, two modulations, three DMRS additional positions and four delay spreads. Result: the neural receiver **gains 1.0–2.0 dB over practical MMSE in 3 of 16 scenarios, ties in 10, underperforms by about 2 dB at QPSK, fails silently at DMRS AddPos = 2, and is architecturally incompatible at 64-QAM**. **[verified]**
Selected numbers (10 %-BLER operating SNR, dB): baseline CDL-C/300 ns/5 Hz 16-QAM — R1 (practical MMSE) 6.4 vs R3 (DeepRx) 6.4; delay spread 30 ns — **14.2 vs 12.3 (+1.9 dB neural gain)**; delay spread 100 ns — **12.3 vs 10.3 (+2.0 dB)**; Doppler 200 Hz — **12.5 vs 11.6 (+0.9 dB)**; **QPSK — R1 0.4 vs R3 2.3 (neural is ~2 dB worse)**; Doppler 500 Hz — R3 12.4 dB while R1 "Fail" (does not reach 10 % BLER within −2…+18 dB); DMRS AddPos = 2 — R1 6.4 dB, R3 "Fail". Their own caveat: standard error on the 10 %-BLER operating SNR is ≈0.3 dB. **[verified]**

### 2.6 Sionna tutorials and the reference 5G NR PUSCH neural receiver

**5G NR PUSCH Neural Receiver** — Sionna Research Kit tutorial — [nvlabs.github.io](https://nvlabs.github.io/sionna/v2.1.0/rk/tutorials/neural_receiver/) — NVIDIA.
Documents the NRX of Wiesmayr et al. replacing channel estimation, equalisation and demapping with a single network operating on a whole 5G NR slot, integrated into the OpenAirInterface stack, with **TensorRT** export and an explicit **sub-1 ms inference latency target**. **[verified]**

**Neural Receiver for OFDM SIMO Systems** — Sionna tutorial — [nvlabs.github.io](https://nvlabs.github.io/sionna/phy/tutorials/notebooks/Neural_Receiver.html) — NVIDIA.
Reference open-source training recipe: post-DFT received grid → LLRs → outer LDPC decoder, baselines are LMMSE with perfect CSI and with LS estimation; "**All the considered end-to-end systems use an LDPC outer code from the 5G NR specification, QPSK modulation, and a 3GPP CDL channel model**". Note that the tutorial configuration is QPSK-only — the default reference recipe does not by itself demonstrate constellation-order generalisation. **[verified]**

### 2.7 Model-based deep learning for deep receivers (Raviv / Shlezinger line)

**Adaptive and Flexible Model-Based AI for Deep Receivers in Dynamic Channels** — IEEE Wireless Communications (2024; arXiv 2023) — [arXiv:2305.07309](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.07309) — Raviv, Park, Simeone, Eldar, Shlezinger.
A magazine article arguing that wireless receiver design "poses unique challenges that fundamentally differ from those encountered in traditional deep learning domains. The main challenges arise from the **limited power and computational resources of wireless devices**, as well as from the **dynamic nature of wireless communications, which causes continual changes to the data distribution**. These challenges impair conventional AI based on highly-parameterized DNNs." They propose rethinking architecture, data, and training algorithms, with **model-based deep learning** for compact networks, meta-learning and **generalized Bayesian learning** for robustness. **[verified]**

**Uncertainty-Aware and Reliable Neural MIMO Receivers via Modular Bayesian Deep Learning** — [arXiv:2302.02436](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.02436) (2023, v4 2024) — Raviv, Park, Simeone, Shlezinger.
The most explicit overconfidence statement I found: "**Conventionally trained DNN-based modules are known to produce poorly calibrated, typically overconfident, decisions.** This implies that an incorrect decision may propagate through the architecture without any indication of its insufficient accuracy." They quantify it: the trained receiver "tends to produce overconfident predictions. This is in the sense that **the confidence level … produced by the receiver is typically larger than the actual test accuracy of the decisions**." Their remedy is Bayesian learning, applied **modularly** — note their finding that end-to-end Bayesian training "only encourages the outputs … to be calibrated, not accounting for the internal modular structure; **the soft estimates learned by the internal modules may still be overconfident**, which does not necessarily improve end-to-end accuracy." They evaluate with reliability diagrams and expected calibration error (ECE) on DeepSIC. **[verified]**

### 2.8 LLR-centric receiver augmentation and estimation

**Neural Augmentation of MIMO-OFDM Receivers for Universal LLR Reconstruction** — [arXiv:2606.29345](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.29345) (June 2026) — Eger & Shlezinger.
A compact, modular element-wise scaled CNN that refines the soft outputs of **any** primary detector — model-based (LMMSE, sphere decoder) or data-driven (DeepRx, DeepSIC) — with no knowledge of what is wrong. It targets two regimes explicitly named in the requirements: "**modulation scaling**" (a detector trained for a fixed modulation, e.g. QPSK, has "limited generalization to other constellations") and "**LLR completion**" (reduced-complexity detectors that compute only a subset of bit LLRs). **[verified]**
Two quantified findings that directly support our problem taxonomy. (a) Missing LLRs: an unaugmented reduced-complexity sphere detector "assigns zero to the missing LLRs, which prevents its MI from rising above 2/3 as expected, **resulting in complete BLER saturation**"; with augmentation MI → 1 and BLER beats LMMSE by **2–4 dB**. (b) Impairments: under clipping, the unaugmented LMMSE has "MI only marginally below its augmented counterpart" yet "**its BLER saturates at an error floor with no waterfall behavior. This highlights that MI alone does not fully predict BLER performance, as the latter is also governed by the LDPC code and graph structure.**" **[verified]**

**Novel Deep Neural OFDM Receiver Architectures for LLR Estimation** — [arXiv:2503.20500](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.20500) (2025) — Karakoca, Çevik, Hökelek, Görçin.
Two architectures (Dual Attention Transformer, DAT; Residual Dual Non-Local Attention Network, RDNLA) that predict bit LLRs directly from received OFDM IQ **with noise variance (in dB) as an additional input** — an explicit architectural answer to SNR generalisation. Trained with a **bit-metric-decoding (BMD) rate** objective and compared using BCE; BER and BLER compared against conventional and existing neural receivers across SNR, with DAT/RDNLA reported to win. Implemented in TensorFlow/Sionna. **[verified]**

### 2.9 Fallback / arbitration architectures

**Hybrid Neural/Traditional OFDM Receiver with Learnable Decider** — IEEE PIMRC 2025 — [arXiv:2509.18574](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.18574) — Obeed & Jian (Huawei).
Trains a lightweight discriminator NN that selects, **per resource grid**, between the DL receiver and the traditional receiver. Motivation stated bluntly: DL receivers "often face challenges such as **poor generalization to unseen channel conditions**" and "their performance heavily depends on the training data distribution and **often fails to generalize** to unseen or dynamic wireless channel conditions". Training uses **18 distinct channel models (6 CDL, 6 TDL, and 2 each of UMi/UMa/RMa)** and deliberately injects "anomalous" samples (rare, hard conditions such as extremely high delay spreads) so the discriminator learns where the neural receiver is weak; results are reported for **4-QAM and 64-QAM** training/evaluation levels. **[verified]**

**Adapting to Reality: Over-the-Air Validation of AI-Based Receivers Trained with Simulated Channels** — IEEE WCNC 2025 — [arXiv:2408.04182](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.04182) — Luostari, Korpi, Honkala, Huttunen (Nokia).
DeepRx trained on 3GPP TS38.901 simulations, validated with **SDR over-the-air** measurements in a small-cell-like environment (average RMS delay spread of the test data 105 ns; "walking" dataset A, "running/swinging" dataset B). Findings are counter-intuitive and directly actionable: DeepRx trained on **LOS channel models (TDL-D, TDL-E, CDL-D, CDL-E) "failed the over-the-air tests despite converging well during training"**, and was inferior to LS/LMMSE in the higher-SINR region; CDL-A also performed poorly; **training on a randomly selected mixture of TDL and CDL models per iteration performed best**; and a model trained over a broad speed range (0–30 m/s) beat models trained at speeds matching the actual walking speed. They conclude that "developing **new channel models tailored for training these learned receivers** would enhance their generalization capability and reduce training time", and caution that their test was limited to a narrow environment. **[verified]**

**When Does a Neural Receiver Help?** (see §2.5) also belongs here: it proposes **detect-and-rollback** — running the neural receiver R3 and a conventional MMSE receiver R1 in parallel and arbitrating per slot with a detector that forwards one of the two LLR streams to the LDPC decoder. A bit-disagreement detector recovers the silent-failure scenario (matching R1 within 0.02 dB) but fails at 500 Hz Doppler; a median-normalised LLR confidence-vote detector captures the neural gain (within 0.06 dB of R3) but partially inherits the silent failure. No single scalar threshold resolves both — the paper shows the tradeoff is structural. **[verified]**

## 3. Reported practical problems

### 3.1 LLR scale mismatch / miscalibration between a neural detector and a conventional LDPC decoder

* **Different LLR streams have different magnitude scales "by construction."** In the detect-and-rollback paper, direct comparison of the neural and conventional LLR magnitudes is declared meaningless: "Direct comparison of |L_R1| and |L_R3| is **not meaningful because the two LLR streams have different magnitude scales by construction**; each stream is therefore normalized by its own median absolute value." They further note that "any subsequent stage that relies on the LLR scale (such as the **LDPC decoder's message scheduling**) operates on the same statistical properties as either R1 or R3 alone." — [arXiv:2605.26157](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26157) **[verified]**
* **Mismatched/masked demapping produces correct signs but wrong magnitudes, and that alone breaks FEC decoding.** "The LLR magnitudes do in general not match the underlying probabilities… **Without additional LLR correction … 'classical' mismatched demapping can lead to severe performance degradation in forward error correction (FEC) decoding.**" — [arXiv:2409.02912](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.02912) **[verified]**
* **Neural detectors emit "raw LLRs" that are not noise-variance scaled, so explicit clipping is mandatory.** "Since recurSIC produces **raw LLRs that are not explicitly scaled by the noise variance**, the absolute LLR values are **clipped to a maximum magnitude L_max**, which is selected per channel model and modulation order but kept constant across SNRs", with L_max chosen as **twice the 95th percentile** of the LLR distribution. LLRs from the fallback bit-flip mechanism are additionally **scaled by α = 0.2 and clipped to 0.1·L_max** "to **limit overconfident soft information** arising from incomplete hypothesis coverage while preserving the sign of the LLRs for reliable decoder operation." Reported clip levels span 0.12 → 2.4 (a ~20× range) purely across channel model and modulation order. — [arXiv:2601.16586](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.16586) **[verified]**
* **Neural modules are systematically overconfident, and the overconfidence is *architectural*, not fixed by end-to-end calibration.** "Conventionally trained DNN-based modules are known to produce **poorly calibrated, typically overconfident, decisions**… the **confidence level produced by the receiver is typically larger than the actual test accuracy**." And on the remedy: end-to-end Bayesian training "only encourages the outputs … to be calibrated, not accounting for the internal modular structure of the architecture. **The soft estimates learned by the internal modules may still be overconfident.**" — [arXiv:2302.02436](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.02436) **[verified]**
* **A high mutual information is not sufficient — the LLR *scale* has to match what the LDPC decoder expects.** Under clipping, an unaugmented LMMSE detector has "MI only marginally below its augmented counterpart", yet "**its BLER saturates at an error floor with no waterfall behavior**. This highlights that **MI alone does not fully predict BLER performance**, as the latter is also governed by the LDPC code and graph structure, as well as the decoding algorithm employed." — [arXiv:2606.29345](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.29345) **[verified]**
* **When the detector is trained for one modulation and run on another, the output is "suboptimally calibrated."** The QPSK scenario: with only Q_d = 2 bits per resource element, "the four output channels of the network produce **two wasted slots per resource element**. The network was trained with 16-QAM as the dominant modulation in the training distribution; **the QPSK output, while functional, is suboptimally calibrated**", costing ~2 dB. — [arXiv:2605.26157](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26157) **[verified]**
* **Remedies actually reported in the literature** (each with its source):
  * *LLR clipping to a per-modulation, per-channel-model maximum* — [arXiv:2601.16586](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.16586); an "LLR clipping parameter λ_max = 20" is also listed among the decoder hyper-parameters in [arXiv:2009.02591](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.02591). **[verified]**
  * *Aggressive down-scaling of low-confidence fallback LLRs* (α = 0.2, clip to 0.1·L_max) — [arXiv:2601.16586](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.16586). **[verified]**
  * *Per-stream median normalisation* before cross-receiver comparison — [arXiv:2605.26157](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26157). **[verified]**
  * *Learned LLR correction as a post-processing module* — [arXiv:2606.29345](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.29345), and (title-level) "Deep Learning Aided LLR Correction Improves the Performance of Iterative MIMO Receivers", IEEE 2023 **[metadata only]**. **[verified]**
  * *EXIT-chart-based degree-profile matching of the code to the learned demapper's LLR statistics, with a stability condition λ₂ ≤ β to avoid an error floor* — [arXiv:1911.13055](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.13055); T-EXIT analysis of learned min-sum decoders in [arXiv:2102.03828](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.03828). **[verified]**
  * *Training with the decoder in the loop so that the decoder shapes the LLR statistics* — [arXiv:1911.13055](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.13055), [arXiv:2609.04004](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.04004) (DUIDD), [arXiv:2510.22608](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.22608). **[verified]**
  * *Modular Bayesian calibration rather than end-to-end calibration* — [arXiv:2302.02436](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.02436). **[verified]**
  * *Use coded BER, not uncoded BER, as the deployment metric* — "Investigating the coded BER reveals whether the LLRs … sufficiently capture the uncertainty of the detected bits for the purposes of LDPC decoding. The coded BER can also be seen as a proxy to the accuracy of the LLRs themselves" — [arXiv:2005.01494](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.01494). **[verified]**
* **What I did *not* find:** an explicit study using **temperature scaling** on a neural receiver's LLRs, or a paper that reports an empirically fitted scalar LLR scaling factor for a *trained 5G-NR neural receiver feeding a real LDPC decoder*. Clipping appears repeatedly; temperature scaling does not appear in any full text I read. Treat this as a genuine gap. **[gap claim]**

### 3.2 Outer-loop / error-floor problems: fine at BER 1e-3, failing at low error rates or at high SNR

* **A neural receiver reaching a BER floor at high SNR, with the authors conceding it "hinders its performance at very low bit error rates."** DeepRx MIMO: "it can nearly match the uncoded BER of the genie-aided LMMSE up to SNRs of **14 dB, after which it seems to encounter a BER floor**"; and in the conclusions, "our future work will include further investigations into the **error floor of the proposed MIMO DeepRx, which hinders its performance at very low bit error rates**." — [arXiv:2010.16283](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.16283) **[verified]** *(This closes the "no paper documents a neural-receiver error floor" gap noted in the companion review.)*
* **A neural receiver that never reaches the target BLER at all in an out-of-distribution configuration.** At DMRS AddPos = 2 the pretrained DeepRx_2M "**BLER floors at 100 percent for every SNR from 4 dB upward**" while the conventional MMSE baseline reaches 0 % BLER at 8 dB and the perfect-CSI bound sits at 6.3 dB. — [arXiv:2605.26157](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26157) **[verified]**
* **The same paper quantifies a hard floor for any bounded LLR-residual correction.** With a safety budget Δ_saf = ln(4) (a 4:1 LLR magnitude budget), the measured **confidently-wrong bit fraction plateaus at ~7 % at high SNR** in the OOD scenario. Proposition 2: for any additive correction r with ‖r‖∞ ≤ Δ_saf, BER(L_R3 + r) ≥ p_cw — so **no bounded residual fix can push BER below ~7 %**; only a discrete switch to a differently-scaled LLR vector escapes it. — [arXiv:2605.26157](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26157) **[verified]**
* **BLER that saturates with no waterfall at all because of LLR scale, while the information-theoretic metric looks fine.** "Its BLER saturates at an error floor **with no waterfall behavior**." — [arXiv:2606.29345](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.29345) **[verified]**
* **High error floor produced by the finetuning recipe itself.** Finetuning the OTA prototype on sequences with BLER worse than ~10⁻² "improved… the low SNR range. However, it also led to a **high error floor in the high SNR range**" because noisy finetuning data "obscures important structure in the data" — [arXiv:1707.03384](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.03384). **[verified]**
* **The classic decoder-side framing of the same problem, and the current state of the art for fixing it.** 5G-NR LDPC codes "suffer from the error floor phenomenon, which hinders the achievement of extremely low FERs"; 6G xURLLC needs FER < 10⁻⁹; the boosted neural min-sum decoder is reported to meet that requirement "without a severe error floor", but only after adding block-wise training, boosting with uncorrected vectors, dynamic weight sharing and transfer learning. — [arXiv:2405.13413](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.13413) **[verified]**
* **A counterexample, for honesty:** in one 5G-NR MU-MIMO neural receiver, "the error floor is **significantly lower** with the neural receiver, suggesting that it implicitly performs a more accurate channel estimation than the two other schemes." — [arXiv:2312.02601](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.02601) **[verified]** The error-floor picture is therefore architecture- and scenario-dependent, not uniformly bad.
* **In the measured regime, a tuned classical chain can still win at the operating point.** On the ETH 5G-NR testbed, "site-specific LMMSE channel estimation … when combined with iterative detection and decoding, achieves **the lowest error rate observed in our datasets**" — better than both the finetuned MDX/NRX neural receivers and the DUIDD receiver. — [arXiv:2609.04004](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.04004) **[verified]**
* **Neural gain is bounded and sometimes negative even in-distribution vs. a *tuned* conventional baseline.** 3 of 16 scenarios gained 1.0–2.0 dB, 10 were statistical ties, and one (QPSK) lost ~2 dB. — [arXiv:2605.26157](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26157) **[verified]**

### 3.3 Constellation-order generalisation (QPSK vs 16-QAM vs 64-QAM) and modulation-agnostic training

* **A hard architectural failure at 64-QAM.** The published `DeepRx_2M` reference model "produces a fixed output tensor with **four channels per resource element**… sufficient for QPSK (2 bits/symbol) and 16-QAM (4), but **not for 64-QAM (6)**. At 64-QAM the bit-LLR extraction step is **architecturally incompatible with the network output**, and the receiver fails to produce usable LLRs at all." — [arXiv:2605.26157](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26157) **[verified]**
* **The known-good design pattern: hierarchical bit masking over the maximum supported order.** DeepRx sets its output width to the highest supported modulation (8 channels, up to 256-QAM) and masks unused bit positions during training and inference, so "**the same output bits/LLRs correspond to the same part of the constellation space, regardless of the modulation used**"; a single network covers QPSK/16/64/256-QAM with no retraining. — [arXiv:2005.01494](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.01494) **[verified]**
* **The same trick, applied dynamically at inference, removes the retraining requirement for MCS changes.** The Var-MCS NRX supports "dynamic modulation and coding scheme (MCS) configurations **without the need for any re-training and without additional inference cost**" by masking unused higher-order LLR outputs — but it explicitly requires LLR correction to be correct (see §3.1). — [arXiv:2409.02912](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.02912) **[verified]**
* **A concrete, quantified failure of a QPSK/16-QAM-trained network on QPSK.** ~2 dB worse than practical MMSE at QPSK, attributed to two of four output channels being wasted and to the training distribution being 16-QAM-dominated. — [arXiv:2605.26157](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26157) **[verified]**
* **Named as an open problem by the Sionna/Bell-Labs authors themselves:** "The flexibility w.r.t. the modulation schemes is an interesting and important challenge. A possible approach … could be in **input embeddings that account for the actual modulation scheme**." — [arXiv:2312.02601](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.02601) **[verified]**
* **Deep-learning receivers "trained for a fixed modulation scheme (e.g., QPSK) … exhibit limited generalization to other constellations"**, which motivates an explicit "modulation scaling" regime in the LLR-augmentation framework. — [arXiv:2606.29345](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.29345) **[verified]**
* **Evidence that modulation is a real generalisation axis in the learned-demapper literature.** The Sionna OFDM reference recipe is QPSK-only and uses a 5G NR LDPC outer code — [Sionna tutorial](https://nvlabs.github.io/sionna/phy/tutorials/notebooks/Neural_Receiver.html); the hybrid-decider work trains and evaluates separately at 4-QAM and 64-QAM using two different training levels — [arXiv:2509.18574](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.18574); the DeepRx MIMO variant fixes 16-QAM only — [arXiv:2010.16283](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.16283). **[verified]**
* **Remedies reported:** hierarchical bit masking ([arXiv:2005.01494](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.01494)), modulation-index input embeddings ([arXiv:2312.02601](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.02601)), noise-variance (dB) as an auxiliary input ([arXiv:2503.20500](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.20500)), post-hoc LLR reconstruction/scaling ([arXiv:2606.29345](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.29345)), and one network trained jointly across MCS with a multi-loss ([arXiv:2409.02912](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.02912)). **[verified]**

### 3.4 SNR generalisation, channel-model mismatch, and the simulation-to-over-the-air gap

* **SNR generalisation is a stated failure mode.** "A **separate NN is trained for each SNR value** as the trained NNs struggle to generalize to other SNR values." — [arXiv:1812.05929](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.05929). And in the foundational paper: "we have observed that **training at certain SNRs or SNR ranges does not translate obviously to test performance. Training at low SNR for instance does not allow for the discovery of higher structure important in higher SNR scenarios.**" — [arXiv:1702.00832](https://arxiv.org/abs/1702.00832). **[verified]**
* **Finetuning at low SNR destroys high-SNR behaviour.** "Finetuning with sequences for which the initial receiver had a performance worse than about 10⁻² resulted in performance improvements in the low SNR range. However, it also led to a high error floor in the high SNR range… **the receiver looses its ability to generalize to high SNR.**" — [arXiv:1707.03384](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.03384) **[verified]**
* **LOS/NLOS mismatch is a real, measured failure — and it is invisible in simulation.** DeepRx models trained on LOS channel models "**failed the over-the-air tests despite converging well during training**" (TDL-D, TDL-E, CDL-D, CDL-E), and were worse than LS/LMMSE in the higher-SINR region; CDL-A also performed poorly. — [arXiv:2408.04182](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.04182) **[verified]**
* **Counter-intuitively, *broad and randomised* training beats *matching* training.** Training on a randomly mixed TDL/CDL set performed best; a model trained at simulated UE speeds spanning 0–30 m/s beat models trained at speeds matching the actual walking speed; an extremely broad delay-spread range (50 ns–5 µs) matched a narrow, environment-matched range. Conclusion: "the crucial role of **rich and randomized training data**", plus the need for "new channel models tailored for training these learned receivers". — [arXiv:2408.04182](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.04182) **[verified]**
* **Channel-model overfitting is explicitly guarded against in the reference implementations.** "A practical challenge is to train the system such that it **does not overfit to the underlying dataset or channel model**. We therefore randomize the channel during training by using system level 3GPP urban microcell (UMi) channel models **with random user drops**." — [arXiv:2312.02601](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.02601). And: "In addition, it is **unclear whether evaluations performed on simplistic channel models generalize to more challenging channel environments**." **[verified]**
* **Out-of-distribution operation is a named open problem across the field.** "Across this literature, evaluation is typically performed **within the training distribution**. Generalization to out-of-distribution operating points is **acknowledged as an open problem**… but is not the focus of the architectural contributions." — [arXiv:2605.26157](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26157). Related: "DL-based receivers often face challenges such as **poor generalization to unseen channel conditions**" — [arXiv:2509.18574](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.18574). **[verified]**
* **The SNR/dataset-mismatch admission in a prediction-based receiver:** "such findings **may not translate well to different channel conditions, as the predictor has been trained on a dataset with a fixed Doppler spread and NTN-TDL-C channel model**." — [arXiv:2502.09326](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.09326) **[verified]**
* **The measured sim-to-real gap is large enough that site-specific finetuning is now standard practice.** On the ETH 5G-NR testbed, site-specific finetuning of a fully-trainable NRX cut dataset BLER by ~1/3 in one scenario and improved effective SNR by 0.54–0.83 dB at 25 % BLER in another; finetuning on one campaign still helped on a campaign >6 months later, but the temporal gap increased BLER — "these observations demonstrate that site-specific finetuning remains effective over time but **motivate continued finetuning after deployment**." — [arXiv:2609.04004](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.04004) **[verified]**
* **The same study reports that the model-driven (least tunable) receiver benefits least from finetuning** ("substantially improves fully trainable and model-driven neural receivers, while resulting in only **marginal gains for the less tunable model-based receiver**"), while the decoder-in-the-loop DUIDD gains only **0.004 absolute BLER** — i.e. **putting the decoder in the loop reduces, rather than increases, what site adaptation can buy you.** — [arXiv:2609.04004](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.04004) **[verified]**
* **Framing of the underlying difficulty** (the reason SNR/channel generalisation is not just a data-augmentation problem): receiver design "poses unique challenges that fundamentally differ from those encountered in traditional deep learning domains… the **dynamic nature of wireless communications … causes continual changes to the data distribution**." — [arXiv:2305.07309](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.07309) **[verified]**

### 3.5 Complexity / latency / training instability of running the decoder jointly

**Training instability and gradients through decoding:**

* **End-to-end training through a 40-iteration unrolled LDPC decoder fails with a naive loss.** "It was experimentally observed that **training the end-to-end system by minimizing (12) leads to poor performance**"; the fix was a multi-loss summing BCE at the demapper output over *all* iterations. — [arXiv:1911.13055](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.13055) **[verified]**
* **Vanishing gradients in deep unrolled decoders, and the standard remedies.** "It may lead to the **vanishing gradient problem** during the training process. These reasons lead to that current neural SP decoders may only support short to moderate codelengths" → **iteration-by-iteration greedy training** — [arXiv:2102.03828](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.03828). "The vanishing gradient problem **impedes the effective training of early iteration weights**. This results in a **consistent test FER up to iteration 40** for ℓ̄ = 50" → **block-wise training schedule** (Δ₁ = 5, Δ₂ = 10) — [arXiv:2405.13413](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.13413). **[verified]**
* **Non-differentiable decoder/detector operations.** Min-sum check nodes have "**non-differentiable kinks**" and require **subgradient descent** — [arXiv:1706.07043](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.07043). The exact Viterbi state-equality criterion "is **non-differentiable**, thus we minimize a multi-class cross entropy loss, **acting as a surrogate loss**" — [arXiv:2009.02591](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.02591). **[verified]**
* **Training through a real channel at all requires gradient estimation with its own variance tradeoff.** σ_w "controls a tradeoff between the accuracy of the loss function gradient approximation and the estimator variance… **reducing σ_w leads to higher variance of the gradient estimator, causing slow convergence**." — [arXiv:1911.13055](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.13055) and [arXiv:1812.05929](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.05929). **[verified]**
* **Decoder-in-the-loop training is only worth it if the extra loss terms are engineered carefully.** DUIDD (decoder in the loop, 30 parameters) needed 3·10³ batches of BCE *followed by* 3·10³ batches of a normalized LogSumExp BLER loss for pretraining, then 10³ BCE + 10³ LogSumExp batches for finetuning. — [arXiv:2609.04004](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.04004) **[verified]**

**Latency and complexity, with hardware:**

* **Apple Silicon, which is directly relevant to this project.** The calibration-drift paper is the only work I found reporting neural-receiver latency on Apple hardware: on a **Mac Studio M3 Ultra (32 CPU cores), CPU mode**, the DeepRx_2M forward pass is **72.10 ms mean (71.64 median)** per slot, LDPC decoding **9.12 ms**, practical MMSE **3.69 ms**; full pipeline R3 = **81.23 ms**, R5 = 85.06 ms. On an NVIDIA Quadro RTX 6000 the forward pass drops to **24.10 ms** (3.0×), LDPC 8.54 ms, full R3 32.64 ms. "On these research platforms [this] is still **well above the 1 ms 5G slot budget** at 15 kHz subcarrier spacing", and closing the gap "is a function of the inference stack — INT8 quantization, kernel fusion, TensorRT, and AI-RAN-optimized hardware". **Note: this measurement is CPU-only, not CoreML/Metal GPU — no published Metal numbers were found.** **[verified]**
* **Compute ratio.** The same paper counts "approximately **1.5 GFLOPs per slot** for the reference architecture at 26 PRB. This is approximately **30 times the FLOP count of the MMSE equalization step**", for 1.23 M parameters. **[verified]**
* **Parameter count inflates the problem at MIMO scale.** The same paper notes the field spans 1.23 M (DeepRx_2M) up to 5–10 M (MIMO variants) and higher for multi-user designs, and that "as the receiver scales… **the latency margin shrinks rapidly**, particularly when multi-user inference, HARQ retransmissions, and higher-order modulation are accounted for." **[verified]**
* **The counter-datapoint that real-time is achievable, at a measured price.** With TensorRT on an **NVIDIA A100**, the NRX hits **< 1 ms** inference, the architecture had to be shrunk until it fit, and the latency constraint costs "**less than 0.7 dB**" SNR versus a non-real-time NRX. Real-time also forbids inter-frame parallelisation ("only intra-frame parallelization can be utilized as buffering would be required otherwise"). — [arXiv:2409.02912](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.02912) **[verified]**
* **The parameter-count / tunability tension is explicit in the measured study.** NRX ~1.2–4.4·10⁵ parameters, MDX ~2.5·10³, DUIDD 30; both NRX depths beat DUIDD on BLER, "but at **much higher complexity**", while DUIDD "has similar complexity to classical IDD and about **twice the detection complexity of the non-iterative baseline**". — [arXiv:2609.04004](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.04004) **[verified]**
* **Latency was still future work in the GLOBECOM 2023 reference receiver** ("We leave it open for future work to optimize the architecture towards real-time inference") — [arXiv:2312.02601](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.02601) — whereas the Sionna PUSCH neural receiver tutorial now targets "**sub-1 ms inference latency**" with TensorRT integration into the OAI stack. **[verified]**
* **The cost of running both detectors:** since LDPC decoding is ~9 ms/slot on their CPU and ~8.5 ms on their GPU, the authors note a CRC-after-decoding rollback architecture "would roughly **double this cost** in the worst case when both streams must be decoded", which is why their arbitration happens *before* decoding. — [arXiv:2605.26157](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26157) **[verified]**

**Measured over the air (real antennas, real channel):**

| Work | Platform | What was actually measured |
|---|---|---|

| Aït Aoudia & Hoydis 2019 ([1812.05929](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.05929)) | USRP B210, 2.3 GHz, 5 MHz, 128 subcarriers | SER of the model-free-trained autoencoder on the real channel |
| Cammerer et al. 2020 ([1911.13055](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.13055)) | 2× USRP B210, 2.35 GHz, 15.94 MHz | **BER of neural IDD with an 802.11n LDPC in the loop**, plus EXIT curves measured from OTA data and an LDPC code optimised on them (0.6 dB demapper gain, 0.2/0.4 dB code gain) |
| Luostari et al. 2024/25 ([2408.04182](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.04182)) | SDR small-cell OTA, walking + running datasets | DeepRx vs LS/LMMSE; the LOS-model failure and the broad-randomised-training win |
| Wiesmayr et al. 2026 ([2609.04004](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.04004)) | ETH Zurich **standard-compliant 5G NR testbed**, COTS O-RUs, COTS UEs, 100 MHz @ 3.45 GHz, 273 PRBs, two campaigns >6 months apart | Dataset BLER of NRX / MDX / **DUIDD (decoder in the loop)** / IDD / MMSE reference, all after real LDPC decoding — the strongest measured evidence in this survey |

**Hardware-in-the-loop but not radiated:** Cammerer et al. 2023 ([2312.02601](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.02601)) validated the MU-MIMO neural receiver through R&S SMW200A + MSR4 with 3GPP conformance-test TDL profiles (217 PRBs, 4 RX antennas). **[verified]**

**Simulation-only:** O'Shea & Hoydis 2017; Nachmani et al.; Lugosch & Gross; Bennatan et al.; Kim et al. (both); Dai et al.; Kwak et al.; Fritschek et al.; Raviv et al. (Viterbi ensemble); Shental & Hoydis; Honkala et al. (DeepRx and DeepRx MIMO); Aït Aoudia & Hoydis 2022 (pilotless); Raviv/Shlezinger lines; Karakoca et al.; Eger & Shlezinger; Obeed & Jian; Fesl & Capar; Jayarathne et al.; Zheng et al.; Abdollahpour et al. — all link-level simulation only.
**Simulation-only but with hardware timing:** Elnashar 2026 ([2605.26157](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26157)) is a pure MATLAB 5G-Toolbox link-level benchmark, but *does* report wall-clock per-slot latency measured on a Mac Studio M3 Ultra **CPU** and an RTX 6000 GPU. **[verified]**

**Bottom line for the team:** the decoder-in-the-loop idea has exactly one strong measured validation (Cammerer et al. 2020, USRP, LDPC, 0.6 dB demapper gain + 0.2–0.4 dB code gain) and exactly one measured *disappointment* (Wiesmayr et al. 2026, real 5G NR testbed: DUIDD finetuning gained only 0.004 absolute BLER, and a site-tuned classical LMMSE + IDD chain had the lowest error rate of all receivers tested). Everything else in this line is simulation.

## 5. Uncertainties

1. **No published CoreML/Metal/ANE latency numbers for a neural receiver.** The only Apple-silicon timing I found is Elnashar 2026's **CPU-mode** M3 Ultra measurement (72.1 ms/slot for a 1.23 M-parameter DeepRx). Whether CoreML on the M-series GPU can reach the ~1 ms 5G slot budget is, as far as this search can tell, **unmeasured in the public literature**. This is the single biggest unknown for the stated deployment target.
2. **The Elnashar benchmark is a single-author preprint on a public MathWorks reference model.** Its numbers are precise and internally consistent, and it uses the LDPC decoder in the loop, but it has not been peer-reviewed as far as I could verify, and it evaluates one architecture (DeepRx_2M) at one carrier/bandwidth/antenna configuration (SIMO 1×2, 26 PRB, 3.5 GHz). Its "1.0–2.0 dB gain" headline should not be generalised to MIMO or to full-band NR without caution.
3. **"DeepRx_2M architecturally incompatible at 64-QAM" is a property of that *reference implementation*, not of DeepRx.** The original DeepRx paper describes 8 output channels supporting up to 256-QAM via bit masking ([2005.01494](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.01494)). Do not report "DeepRx cannot do 64-QAM."
4. **The LLR-correction paper (Chen & Wang, IEEE 2023, Xplore 10246845) was not read.** Title, venue and authors are metadata-verified only. Any claim about its gains would be unverified; I deliberately assert none.
5. **"Temperature scaling" as an LLR remedy: not found.** I saw clipping, median normalisation, per-stream scaling, learned LLR correction, and Bayesian modular calibration, but no paper applying temperature scaling to a neural receiver's LLRs. Either it is obvious-and-unpublished, or it does not work in this setting. Marked as a gap rather than a finding.
6. **Error-floor evidence at BLER 1e-5 / 1e-6 with a decoder in the loop remains thin.** DeepRx MIMO documents a *BER* floor around 14 dB SNR and explicitly defers investigation; the calibration-drift paper's floor is a catastrophic 100 % BLER OOD failure, not a subtle error floor. **No paper I read plots neural-receiver BLER down to 1e-5 as a deliberate error-floor study.** The error-floor machinery exists on the *decoder* side (Kwak et al. 2025) but has not, to my knowledge, been combined with a full neural detector.
7. **Simulation-to-real direction of the gap is not settled.** Luostari et al. find that *broader, more randomised* training beats *matched* training; Wiesmayr et al. find that *site-specific finetuning* gives large, repeatable gains. These are not contradictory (broad pretraining + site finetuning is consistent with both), but the field has not converged on a single prescription, and both studies are single-lab.
8. **The Raviv "Adaptive and Flexible" article is a magazine piece**, so its claims are positions and design recommendations rather than new measurements. I have cited it only for framing.
9. **I could not retrieve Sionna's "Introduction to Iterative Detection and Decoding" tutorial text** (the page did not yield extractable content in this environment), so its "Optimizing IDD with Machine Learning" discussion — referenced in the companion review — is not independently verified here.
10. **Channel-model coverage in the measured studies is narrow.** Luostari et al. explicitly warn their testing "was limited to a relatively narrow environment"; Wiesmayr et al. cover two indoor scenarios. Neither covers outdoor macro mobility, which is where the neural receiver's claimed high-Doppler advantage (Elnashar's +0.9 dB at 200 Hz) would matter most.
